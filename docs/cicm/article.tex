\titlerunning{Formalising Grothendieck's Working Notes}
\author{Michel Hua\orcidID{0009-0002-8304-0683}}

\begin{abstract}
The University of Montpellier holds the mathematical working notes of Alexandre Grothendieck: 178 openly accessible folders, 16,074 digitised pages that its inventory dates from 1953 to 1991. An edition in progress has transcribed \cntPages{} of these pages with a large language model and restated \cntHandFolders{} folders in current notation. Statements drawn from those restatements were then formalised in Lean~4 against a pinned revision of Mathlib, in \cntFiles{} files (\cntFolderFiles{} folders and two Bourbaki drafts) holding \cntTheorems{} top-level theorems that depend only on Lean's three standard axioms; \cntCards{} cards give each formalised statement its page and a verdict. We describe the pipeline from page to theorem, the rule that ties each theorem to a page without attributing to Grothendieck what the edition supplied, and a triage of 203 open statements by reachability. The formalisation found false glosses in the machine readings, claims on the pages that it refutes, slips that it corrects, and statements already in print. For one table of the notes it proves the trivialising exponents for every~$n$ in a homogeneous model, modulo a dictionary it does not formalise; the Picard groups involved were already in print for $n$ even. We set out its relation to seven conjectures that appear in the notes, report how Mathlib received the general lemmas proposed to it, and state the limits of human checking: a proof shows that a reading holds together, but not that the reading is what the page says.
\keywords{Lean \and Mathlib \and autoformalisation \and manuscripts \and language models \and Grothendieck}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Alexandre Grothendieck gave his mathematical papers to his former student Jean Malgoire in 1990 and 1995. They were deposited at the University of Montpellier in 2010 and given to it in 2012. The university has catalogued the fonds and placed 178 folders, 16,074 pages by the inventory's count, in open access as facsimiles \cite{montpellier}. They are working notes: drafts in ink and pencil, restarted, struck and annotated, in a notation of his own. Mathematicians have edited twenty folders by hand, among them \emph{Les Dérivateurs} \cite{kmm} and the first volume of \emph{À la poursuite des champs} \cite{maltsiniotis2022}; the rest had not been transcribed.

Since August 2026 an edition has read the remaining folders with a large language model as first transcriber \cite{huajtei,repo}. At the commit this paper describes\footnote{Commit \cntSnapshot{} of the repository \cite{repo}, \cntSnapshotDate. Every count in the paper is taken at that commit, most of them by \texttt{docs/cicm/figures.mjs}, which writes them into the source of the paper.}, \cntBatches{} passes had read \cntPages{} pages in \cntHandFolders{} folders into a restricted \LaTeX{} with a critical apparatus, and every one of the \cntHandFolders{} folders had a \emph{modernised reading}: a restatement in current notation, in French, derived from the transcription and never from the hand. A companion paper for the \emph{Journal of the TEI} \cite{huajtei} covers the encoding of the transcriptions and the checking of the readings; this one covers the formalisation of the statements. The rights in the manuscripts belong to Grothendieck's heirs. Following the reply of an institution consulted in September 2026, the reading views of the transcriptions are withheld from the public site until the heirs authorise them; the statements, the Lean files and the written-out proofs remain public, and each cites the page of Montpellier's facsimile.

A modernised reading claims that a stretch of manuscript says a particular piece of mathematics; a model makes the claim and no mathematician has checked it. Such a reading usually fails in one of four ways: an inverted variance, a hypothesis the page carries and the reading drops, an inclusion turned into an equality, or a modern name applied to something it does not fit. A proof assistant refuses all four. Lean cannot say whether the reading is faithful to the page, because a misreading can be formalised as exactly as a correct reading. It can say whether what the reading states holds, under which hypotheses, and with which counterexample when it does not.

The contributions are: (i) a pipeline from facsimile to Lean theorem, with a rule of attribution linking each theorem to a page (Sections~\ref{sec:pipeline} and~\ref{sec:attribution}); (ii) a triage of the open statements by what Mathlib can reach (Section~\ref{sec:triage}); (iii) an account of what the formalisation revealed about the readings and the pages, and of what it can do with the conjectures in the notes (Sections~\ref{sec:found} and~\ref{sec:conj}); (iv) a record of the reception of the derived lemmas by Mathlib, and of what the edition contributes to the Tau Ceti roadmaps (Sections~\ref{sec:mathlib} and~\ref{sec:tauceti}); (v) an account of human checking and of costs (Sections~\ref{sec:checking} and~\ref{sec:costs}).

\section{Corpus and Pipeline}\label{sec:pipeline}

\input{fig-pipeline.tex}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the chain. The transcription is written twenty pages at a time in a \LaTeX{} subset with nine apparatus macros (illegible, uncertain, supplied, added, struck, note and the like); two scripts refuse any construct outside the subset. The modernised reading of a folder is written in one pass over all its batches, with footnotes that carry what the apparatus carried. A further register, the \emph{findings} (\texttt{src/content/findings.ts}), lists statements of the folders that may not stand in the literature, each written as a claim about the literature with what was searched and what would settle it. It holds \cntFindings{} rows from \cntFindingFolders{} folders: \cntUnsearched{} unsearched, \cntCandidates{} candidates, \cntMatched{} matched, \cntRefuted{} refuted and \cntConfirmed{} confirmed, a status only a person may set. The language models used at each step, and their roles, are listed in the declaration at the end of the paper.

Statements are taken from the readings and the findings and formalised in \texttt{lean/Grothendieck/}, one file per folder (\lean{Folder19.lean}, \lean{Folder161\_2.lean}), plus two files, \lean{Bourbaki288.lean} and \lean{Bourbaki457.lean}, for drafts of the Bourbaki archive \cite{ahpbourbaki}. Each file opens with a docstring that names the folder, the pages and what is and is not proved, and keeps its own namespace. There are \cntFiles{} files (\cntFolderFiles{} folders and the two Bourbaki drafts), with \cntLines{} lines and \cntTheorems{} top-level \lean{theorem} declarations, counted by \texttt{grep -c '\textasciicircum theorem'} over these files, the Bourbaki drafts included. This count measures size, not statements: it includes auxiliary results and the edition's own lemmas and counterexamples, and it leaves out \cntLemmas{} top-level \lean{lemma} declarations, \cntPrivateTheorems{} private theorems, and the \cntUpstreamDecls{} declarations of the general lemmas that the proofs share, kept apart in \cntUpstreamFiles{} files under \texttt{lean/Grothendieck/Upstream/} (Section~\ref{sec:mathlib}). Table~\ref{tab:counts} sets it beside the statements that the appendix attributes. The first file was committed on \cntFirstCommit, and \cntCommits{} commits had touched \texttt{lean/} by the commit described here.

Mathlib is pinned to a revision of its \texttt{master} branch, not to a release, with the toolchain \texttt{leanprover/lean4:v4.35.0-rc4}, so that the proofs are checked against the library the derived pull requests target (Section~\ref{sec:mathlib}).\footnote{Mathlib commit \nolinkurl{715a03a90adf491de40fbec8b456c179a623435f}.} Moving the pin is a deliberate step: a deprecation fails the build, and the files are rebuilt one at a time because each process importing Mathlib needs about 2\,GB. A continuous integration workflow (\texttt{.github/workflows/lean.yml}) builds the library with warnings as errors, so that a \lean{sorry} fails it, and then runs the axiom audit of \texttt{lean-action}. The audit covers the declarations under the project's root namespace, \lean{Grothendieck}, and fails if one of them depends on an axiom other than \lean{propext}, \lean{Classical.choice} and \lean{Quot.sound}; \lean{sorry} and \lean{native\_decide} count as such axioms. The general lemmas of \texttt{Upstream/} are declared in Mathlib's namespaces, so they are audited only through the theorems that use them. The independent kernel re-check, \texttt{leanchecker}, is not run in CI, because it stopped the runner. No file declares an axiom or uses \lean{native\_decide}.

\section{Attribution and the Link from Page to Theorem}\label{sec:attribution}

A formalisation of working notes must keep apart three things: what the page states, what the reading states, and what the formaliser had to add. The edition's rule is that a statement is labelled \q{Grothendieck, page~$N$} only if that page states it. A result the edition established to make a statement provable (a lemma, a weaker hypothesis that suffices, a counterexample) gets a neutral label and never his name. The same rule governs the pull requests to Mathlib: Grothendieck is named only for statements genuinely his, in one line of the description, never in a docstring.

Every proof has three human-readable forms. The Lean file is the record. A card in \texttt{src/content/proofs.ts}, rendered on the site, gives the folder, the batch and page, the reading's name for the statement, the statement, what the formalisation found, and a verdict from a closed list. A card whose statements fare differently carries one verdict for each, as \cntSeveralVerdicts{} do. Of the \cntCards{} cards, \cntHolds{} carry \emph{holds}, \cntMoreGenerally{} \emph{holds, more generally}, \cntPartly{} \emph{partly proved}, \cntDropped{} \emph{the reading dropped a hypothesis}, \cntGlossFalse{} \emph{holds; the gloss was false}, \cntFalsePage{} \emph{false on the page} and \cntConjRefuted{} \emph{conjecture of the page refuted}. The third form is an appendix in French \cite{hualean}, written for a reader who is a mathematician but not a Lean user: for each folder, the leaf in context, the statement as the reading gives it, a proof written out with its intuition, remarks, and what is not proved. Its \cntAppendixPages{} pages are divided into parts that follow the themes of the fonds in chronological order (Figure~\ref{fig:themes}), and the general lemmas are cited by their file in the repository instead of being proved again. The appendix labels each boxed statement by the rule above (Table~\ref{tab:counts}). A theorem is labelled \q{Grothendieck, page~$N$} when the page states it, \q{d'après la page~$N$} when the proof establishes more than the page, and \q{énoncé de l'édition} when the edition found it; a counterexample refutes a claim of the page or of the reading; the other statements of the readings, proved in part or in a model, keep the name \emph{énoncé}. Where a formalisation bears on a finding, the finding's row records it, and a counterexample moves the row to \emph{refuted} with what still stands.

\section{Triage: What Mathlib Reaches}\label{sec:triage}

The first eighteen folders were chosen by hand (issue \#26) as those whose readings would repay a proof. In October 2026 the 203 findings that were still open and had no proof, in 111 folders, were sorted by reachability (issue \#54). Tier~1, reachable now with Mathlib as it stands, held 16; tier~2, a project of a few hundred lines in which part of the statement is reachable, 39; tier~3, out of reach, 147; and one finding was ill-posed as read and set aside to be disproved. A tier says nothing about whether a statement is new.

Most of the corpus is in tier~3 because its statements live in theories that Mathlib does not yet have, or has only in foundational form: schemes beyond their definition and first properties, étale and $\ell$-adic cohomology, crystalline cohomology, topoi and their morphisms, stacks and gerbes, derivators, motives, and Hodge structures on cohomology. Figure~\ref{fig:themes} shows the effect. Four parts of the appendix (motives, Hodge theory, étale and $\ell$-adic cohomology, $p$-divisible groups) contain no proved statement at all, and descent only the field case of a lemma (folder 128), while combinatorial topology, polyhedra, group schemes taken in coordinates, and homotopy theory through test categories carry most of the theorems. The distribution follows Mathlib's coverage; Grothendieck's own centre of gravity in these folders lies in the parts that are empty.

What can be reached is often a special case or a model. A group scheme is treated on $R$-points in coordinates over an arbitrary ring (folder 72), a statement about schemes is proved for $\operatorname{Spec} A$ with $A$ Noetherian (folder 42), and a remark about connections is proved on the trivial bundle only (folder 93). Of the \cntCards{} cards, \cntPartly{} say \emph{partly proved}, and each says what is not. Several others prove a statement only in a model, or in the special case that Mathlib reaches, and say so. We count these as partial too, since a proof in a model checks that the axioms of the page are consistent and that a computation is right without proving the general statement. The issues that track the remaining rows (\#59, \#72) give each an estimated size and a value, and drop those whose only reachable part would be a special case of the edition's choosing.

\section{What Formalisation Revealed}\label{sec:found}

\paragraph{False glosses in the readings.} Most often the page is right and the reading, where it goes beyond the page, is wrong. In folder 158 the Proposition holds as the leaf states it, finiteness being used exactly where the page says \q{comme $E_0$ est fini}, but the modern gloss called an element a left zero and the Rees kernel a point; in the monoid $\{1,a,b\}$ with $xa = a$ and $xb = b$ both $a$ and $b$ qualify, the kernel is $\{a,b\}$, and the element is a right zero, not unique. In folder 63 the reading took a sentence of page 99 that begins in mid-phrase to say that four components of a modular curve share their tangent at a point over $\mathbb F_4$; the four tangents are pairwise distinct. In folder 114 the reading dropped the page's \q{satisfying Loc~4)} from the hypotheses of a statement on fundamental localisers, without which the inclusion $W \subseteq W_0$ fails. In folder 74 it left out an axiom written in the margin, whose end is illegible: under a reconstruction of the axiom the reduction of pages 92--103 holds, and without it the claim of page 97 fails for a Latin square of order~2. In folder 16 the reading's universal ring $\Phi_0 = \mathbb Z[L_0, \Lambda_0]$ does not exist over $\mathbb Z$ for $n \geq 2$, since $\xi_0^n$ is divisible by $n!$; over $\mathbb Q$ the obstruction disappears. In folder 156-3 a footnote's form of the betweenness relation is false when the extremities are incomparable, as the product order on $\mathbb N^2$ shows; the lemma of the page never meets that case. Each reading was corrected. A related error was found without Lean: the summary of folder 114 had presented as true Proposition~4 of page~350 of \emph{À la poursuite des champs}, which the literature refutes by the category of cubes \cite{cisinski2006,maltsiniotis2005}.

\paragraph{Errors on the page.} We call an error on the page a claim written on the leaf, not added by the reading, that Lean refutes or corrects. The \cntFalsePage{} cards marked \emph{false on the page} each refute such a claim by a counterexample in Lean. In folder 19 a comonadicity theorem gives as necessary and sufficient a condition that is only sufficient: the inclusion of the pairs of sets $(X,Y)$ with $Y \neq \emptyset \Rightarrow X \neq \emptyset$ is coreflective, hence comonadic, yet fails the condition. The reading had repeated the claim and identified the condition, wrongly, with Beck's; on page~7 the proviso \q{ou $I$ fini} is false as well. In folder 80 a theorem on crucial arrangements of pseudolines fails for six straight lines, the sides of a triangle and one further line through each vertex. In folder 90 a parenthesis on flat affine groups over a Dedekind ring fails without finite type, for the algebra $\{f \in K[T] : f(0) \in R\}$ over a discrete valuation ring~$R$. In folder 63 the splitting asserted on page~99 is not stable under the Hesse involution, which acts as a transvection. In folder 118 the remark of page~35 that a condition is \q{visiblement stable par sommes} fails for the sum of two points. In folder 150 the equivalence of page~19 needs the strictness condition circled at the foot of page~16. In folder 158 the criterion of page~52 fails, as written, for the discrete category on two objects. In folder 160 a corollary that leaves a sign undetermined fails for $p$ and $n$ odd: over $\mathbb F_3$ with $n = 1$ there are two trivialisations to count, but no square root of $a_0 = -1$; with the sign $(-1)^n$ it holds. One card refutes a conjecture: in folder 156-4 the saturated parts that page~68 presumes to form a Boolean algebra form a non-distributive hexagon in a seven-element model. Four further errors are slips rather than false claims: an index and a label carried to the wrong place (folder 86), a head line that contradicts the table below it (folder 152), a translation that is wrong in characteristic~2 (folder 70), and a normalisation $ad - bc = 1$ added on page~121 of folder 93, which Lean refutes, since the determinant of the homography is a unit that need not be a square ($s = 2t$ over $\mathbb F_5$). The edition records all of these as errors in notes he wrote for himself.

\paragraph{Statements already in print.} Formalisation often sends the edition back to the literature. In folder 16 the statement that the Lefschetz-type conjecture follows from $D(X)$ and the existence of \emph{some} algebraic isomorphism $H^{2n-i}(X) \to H^i(X)$ holds by Cayley--Hamilton in Lean. The register had listed it as possibly unpublished; the row is now \emph{matched} to Kleiman's Theorem~2.9 of 1968 \cite{kleiman1968}, proved by the same argument, and to Theorem~4-1 of his 1994 survey \cite{kleiman1994}.

In the other direction, a page of the notes can bear on a published result. On page~35 of folder 118 Grothendieck asks whether stability of $\mathcal W_A$ under finite products makes $A$ aspherical, and answers no with a groupoid, for which $\mathcal W_A$ is the isomorphisms. Set beside Cisinski's Proposition~4.3.2 \cite{cisinski2006}, the example shows that condition~(g) of that proposition does not imply condition~(c) for $A = BG$. Lean checks this in a 1-truncated model, which takes as the definition of a weak equivalence of groupoids the usual criterion: a bijection on connected components and an isomorphism on automorphism groups. In a public answer on MathOverflow, Cisinski confirmed that (g)~$\Rightarrow$~(c) needs a further assumption \cite{cisinskimo2026}.

\paragraph{A question of the notes, settled in a model.} Page~70 of folder 146 tabulates, for the moduli spaces $M_{0,n}$, the trivialising exponents of the line bundles $L_i$ and $L = L_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes L_n$ under the symmetric group $\mathfrak S_n$, with question marks against several entries. For $n = 4$ Lean checks the cocycle computation behind the table: on $U = \mathbb P^1 \setminus \{0,1,\infty\}$ the six anharmonic substitutions act through $\mathfrak S_3$, the derivative at the moving point is a 1-cocycle whose sixth power is the coboundary of $\lambda^4(1-\lambda)^4$, and no smaller power is the coboundary of any nowhere-zero function, regular or not; for the cocycle $\operatorname{sgn} g \cdot g'(\lambda)$ of $L$ the exponent is the page's~3. The identification of the equivariant Picard group with $H^1(\mathfrak S_3, \mathcal O(U)^*)$, the reduction from $\mathfrak S_4$ to $\mathfrak S_3$ and the formula for the action on $L$ come from the reading and are not formalised. For $n = 5$, where the page writes \q{12\,?} for both, Lean proves at the level of cocycles that $L$ has order~4 and $L_i$ order~12: the page's 12 is right for $L_i$ and not minimal for~$L$. For $n = 6$ Lean proves the orders 5 and 20, against the page's \q{20\,?} and \q{60\,?}; the trivialising functions come from exact linear algebra on the nine units of $M_{0,6}$, the lower bounds from fixed points where the factor is a primitive fifth root of unity or~$i$. For every $n$, Lean then proves the general formula in a homogeneous model, over a field with primitive roots of unity of orders $n-1$ and $n-2$: $L$ has order $n-1$ and $L_i$ order $(n-1)(n-2)$. In that model a point of $\mathbb P^1$ is a vector of $K^2$, and a trivialisation of $L_i^{\otimes m}$ is a nowhere-zero function on configurations, not required to be regular, invariant under relabelling, of weight $t^{2m}$ in the $i$-th vector and $\det(g)^m$ under $\mathrm{GL}_2(K)$. The trivialisations are products of brackets $[v_i\,v_j]$, a squared Vandermonde product for $L$; the lower bounds come from the configurations $\{0\} \cup \mu_{n-1}$ and $\{0,\infty\} \cup \mu_{n-2}$, rotated by $z \mapsto \omega z$. The dictionary between equivariant line bundles and these functions is not formalised, so the table is settled modulo that dictionary. The groups themselves are known for $n$ even. The quotient $[M_{0,n}/\mathfrak S_n]$ is then the stack of projective classes of smooth binary forms of degree~$n$ modulo $\mathrm{PGL}_2$, whose Picard group is $\mathbb Z/2(n-1)$ when the characteristic does not divide~$n$, by Gorchinskiy and Viviani's Theorem~3.6 \cite{gorchinskiyviviani2008}. Edidin and Hu, from Pernice's presentation by a Borel subgroup \cite{pernice2022}, compute the integral Chow ring of $[M_{0,2g+2}/\mathfrak S_{2g+1}]$, binary forms with a marked root, as $\mathbb Z[l]/(2g(2g+1)\,l)$ for $g \geq 2$, with an explicit generator $l$ \cite[Theorem~1.3]{edidinhu2025}; that $L_i$ generates this group follows from the Lean lower bound. For $n$ odd the groups can be computed by the method of Arsie and Vistoli \cite{arsievistoli2004}; the edition has recomputed them without finding them stated, and its computation is not checked. What the edition adds is the exponents for $n$ odd and the certification of all the exponents in Lean, in the model.

\paragraph{A refutation outside Lean.} Bourbaki's draft no.~307 (1958) on universes \cite{ahpbourbaki} asks whether there are universes other than the empty one and the $U_n$, and answers that it seems probable. Under the first definition given there, with every object in some $U_n$, the answer is no; under the second, which the text says comes to the same thing, the answer is not decided in ZFC with those axioms, since the two definitions differ at every inaccessible cardinal that is a limit of inaccessibles. The argument, which uses the classification of universes as $V_\kappa$ under foundation \cite{williams1969}, is written out in the edition's register and not formalised: Mathlib has no theory of Grothendieck universes in this form. By contrast, two Bourbaki drafts are formalised: no.~288, on the exponential law for compact convergence, and a refutation of Corollary~6 of Proposition~14 of no.~457 (1965), which equates the degree of an integral separable algebra over a field with its number of points. In infinite degree it fails: the locally constant functions on the Cantor set have countable degree and a continuum of points.

None of the seven was in Google DeepMind's \emph{Formal Conjectures} repository \cite{formalconjectures} when the edition searched it on 17 October 2026. That repository collects open conjectures, and some solved variants, stated in Lean with a cited source (the literature, problem lists, Wikipedia, MathOverflow, the OEIS), and the edition's note on conjectures was posted on the Lean community's channel for it. Three of the seven cannot be stated in Mathlib today, because the objects they speak of do not exist there: a Weil cohomology theory, Hodge structures on the cohomology of a variety, the étale fundamental group. A faithful statement needs those definitions first, and one written with ad hoc substitutes would be a different conjecture. The other four can be stated only in part. The edition instead proves the parts around a conjecture that rest on existing theories, and states the conjecture in prose beside them.

Folder 93 shows the method. Pages 193--194 make a remark and ask a question. If a connection defined over the integers has finite monodromy, its reductions modulo~$p$ have vanishing $p$-curvature for almost all~$p$; is the converse true? That converse is the Grothendieck--Katz $p$-curvature conjecture. Lean proves, over a commutative ring of characteristic~$p$ with a derivation $D$, that $D^p$ is again a derivation, Jacobson's formula for the $p$-th power of $D + \mu_a$, and the easy direction on the trivial bundle: in rank one, a connection trivialised by a unit has vanishing $p$-curvature, and in rank~$n$, when $D^p = 0$, so does a connection with a horizontal basis (\lean{curvMat\_eq\_zero\_of\_horizontal}), with worked examples in rank two, the Airy connection among them. The appendix then states the conjecture in full, with its known cases, Katz's for Gauss--Manin connections \cite{katz1972}, and the solvable case through Bost and others \cite{bost2001}, and says that Lean does not touch the general case. Around the Galois action on Teichmüller towers, Lean proves the lemma of page~22 of folder 140-1, that the $\mathfrak S_3$-equivariant Picard group of $M_{0,3}$, $H^1(U_{0,3}, \mathfrak S_3; \mathbb G_m)$, is $\mathbb Z/6$ in a model of the units of $U_{0,3}$, and the entries of folder 146 above. Around the standard conjectures, the Kleiman match of folder 16 is formal; the step of the same folder that splits the critical-dimension condition $A^0$ into two one-sided restriction conditions $A'^0$ and $A''^0$ was not found in Kleiman's 1968 paper and stays a candidate.

The notes also pose questions with no printed source known to the edition, such as a description of the graph of the 27 lines on a cubic surface around a marked triangle as a torsor under a Baer product of quaternion extensions (folder 69), or a bound on the cohomological dimension of affine schemes of mixed characteristic, with residue characteristic~$\ell$, by the degree of imperfection (folder 31). Such questions would need a citable source to enter \emph{Formal Conjectures}. For folder 69 the group-theoretic part is in Lean: the automorphisms preserving a triangle form a group of order $1152$, checked through a stabiliser chain. For folder 31 the local form holds in a sharper form on an open of the spectrum of an excellent henselian local domain, by Gabber and Orgogozo \cite[Theorem~1.2 and §6]{gabberorgogozo2008}, and Mathew bounds the cohomological dimension of the residue fields of $R[1/p]$ for $R$ an excellent normal domain henselian along an ideal containing~$p$ \cite[Proposition~4.5]{mathew2021}. For an arbitrary affine scheme the best general bound we found is the cruder $\dim X + \sup_x \operatorname{cd}_p k(x)$ \cite[Corollary~3.29]{clausenmathew2021}, and the global bound with its \q{${}+3$} was not found in print.

\section{Upstreaming to Mathlib}\label{sec:mathlib}

General lemmas that the proofs needed, and that Mathlib lacked, were proposed to it as pull requests from the author's account: 39 between 10 and 20 October 2026, all carrying the label that Mathlib asks for on LLM-generated contributions. Not all come from this edition: the tracking issue (\#52) records some from a separate exercise in learning Lean. Those from the edition include the (co)monadicity theorem stated as an equivalence (folder 19), additive functors on the Karoubi envelope (113), fixed points of idempotent adjunctions (161-1), stable range one for semilocal rings (79), the Cauchy--Binet formula for exterior powers (1), and right zeros in finite monoids (158).

None was merged. Thirty-six were closed by Mathlib's triage account with a standard message saying that they did not meet the community's standards for the use of AI \cite{mathlibai} and that reviewer time is given first to people who can learn through review; the author closed the other three. Two of these, on direct categories and on the semi-simplex category, were closed by the author after Joël Riou wrote that the first would need significant work to provide a good API, that the second took the wrong approach (a Reedy structure on the semi-simplex category should be restricted from one on the full simplex category), and that he was not enthusiastic about reviewing AI-generated pull requests. Before the closures, Eric Wieser reviewed the pull request on the symmetric Pascal matrix and asked that auxiliary facts be stated as theorems, which was done.

A lemma proved for a folder is shaped by the folder, whereas Mathlib wants an API: the general form, the right generality, and names. A model can produce a correct proof faster than a reviewer can read it, and the community has said that it does not want to spend review on contributions whose author will not learn from the review. The lemmas now live in the repository, in the \cntUpstreamFiles{} files under \texttt{lean/Grothendieck/Upstream/} that the proofs import, and a roadmap proposed to the Tau Ceti project specifies them (Section~\ref{sec:tauceti}) for contributors who write them to Mathlib's conventions. A pull request to Mathlib is opened only after the lemma has been checked against those conventions, and new theories are discussed on Zulip first.

\section{Contributing to the Tau Ceti Roadmaps}\label{sec:tauceti}

Tau Ceti \cite{tauceti} is a Lean~4 library downstream of Mathlib that accepts code written by AI systems. People steer it through a separate repository of roadmaps \cite{taucetiroadmap}. A roadmap is a \texttt{README.md} that specifies one area as an ordered set of definitions and statements, usually with a file \texttt{Suggested.lean} of target signatures whose theorems are left as \lean{sorry}. People write and approve the roadmaps, and the proofs are written in the code repository, by provers among others. Axiom's formalisation of the classification of finite simple groups \cite{axiomcfsg} is one such user: its Lake manifest pins Tau Ceti at a fixed revision.\footnote{\nolinkurl{github.com/AxiomMath/CFSG}, file \texttt{lake-manifest.json}, read on 27 October 2026.}

The author has proposed four roadmaps written from the edition's needs, all still under review on 28 October 2026: the profinite Grothendieck--Teichmüller group (\#1058; folders 119, 140-1, 142, 145, 146 and 147), Lefschetz structures and the standard conjectures (\#1061; folders 14 and 16), the homotopy theory of small categories, with test categories, basic localisers, derivators and Picard stacks (\#1521; folders 76, 104, 106, 107, 114 and 118), and pseudoline arrangements and oriented matroids (\#1523; folders 72, 79, 80, 86 and 154). Each names its folders and pages, and the Lean file and theorem of the edition where one exists, under the rule of Section~\ref{sec:attribution}.

A roadmap fed by such an edition offers a prover five things that a roadmap written from textbooks does not. The first is \emph{targets}: exact statements, each with its page, that were never printed; \#1058, for instance, states as a target the trivialising exponents of folder 146, p.~70, for every~$n$. The second is \emph{proof plans}: a draft gives the order of the lemmas, as folder 16, pp.~47--48, does for the linear algebra of the Lefschetz operator in \#1061. The third is \emph{dead ends}: statements that the formalisation refuted (Section~\ref{sec:found}) enter the roadmap as checks with their counterexamples, such as the classifying-space counterexample recorded under the asphericity of presheaves in \#1521 (folder 118, pp.~33--35), so that no search is spent on them. The fourth is \emph{building blocks}: the edition's compiled theorems and the general lemmas of \texttt{Upstream/}, which a contributor can restate in Tau Ceti instead of proving again. The fifth is \emph{provenance}: the roadmap says who stated what and where, so that a result proved later from it can be attributed correctly.

These roadmaps reach the layers underneath Grothendieck's large conjectures, not the conjectures themselves. The standard conjectures, the anabelian conjectures and the section conjecture need étale cohomology, topoi and motives, which neither Mathlib nor Tau Ceti has yet, and the four roadmaps specify foundations, examples and counterexamples on the way there. The same work is planned for the unpublished drafts of the Bourbaki archive and for Quillen's notebooks (issue \#95). Only statements and page references go upstream, never transcriptions, whose rights belong to others.

\section{Human Checking and Its Limits}\label{sec:checking}

The formalisation checks the readings; it checks neither the transcriptions nor the link between them. The transcriptions are spot-checked. A page of the site, \texttt{/review/}, turns the edition's checklist into single questions, each on one spot of one page, shown beside Montpellier's facsimile with a fixed set of answers; answers come back as issues in the repository and are applied at source, with a dated revision line. The register of answers (\nolinkurl{src/content/review-answered.json}) records \cntReviewItems{} items answered through \cntReviewIssues{} issues. In \cntReviewSettled{} the answer was applied or confirmed the entry as it stood; the others found the spot still illegible or left it open. The settled items include \cntReviewDates{} dates checked on their leaves and \cntReviewInsertions{} insertions whose hand was in question. Other points the author has checked against the facsimile are recorded, dated, in the batch files they concern. No batch has been read whole by a person.

One answer set shows the limit. In issue \#69 the reader, who reads the hand, answered \q{can't tell} to the items that asked whether one of the edition's own arguments holds or whether a reference cited from memory is right, and most of the confirmations given earlier on such items were withdrawn. Reading the hand does not equip a reader to judge the mathematics, and a Lean proof covers part of that need for the statements it formalises. For the faithfulness of a statement to its page, and for everything in tier~3, the edition has no check but a specialist's, and labels its machine second readings as such.

\section{Costs and Reproducibility}\label{sec:costs}

The repository records costs in shares of a weekly usage allowance of the author's subscription, not in tokens or currency, and only for some work. A second transcription pass costs about 1\% of a week per twenty-page batch (issue \#51), and twenty-one batches of Quillen's notebooks were estimated at about 10\% (\#42). For the Lean work no cost per proof is recorded. Its scale is visible in the history: \cntCommits{} commits touching \texttt{lean/} from \cntFirstCommit{} to \cntSnapshotDate, issue estimates of 80 to more than 1,200 lines per finding (\#54, \#72), and rows dropped when the estimate grew beyond their value. The constraint in practice was the weekly allowance, which bounded how many proofs ran in parallel. The code and the site are released under CC0. At commit \cntSnapshot{} the Lean files build with \texttt{cd lean \&\& lake exe cache get \&\& lake build}, which fetches the pinned Mathlib; the axiom audit runs in CI, and \lean{\#print axioms} gives the axioms of any one theorem.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

Mathlib \cite{mathlib2020} on Lean~4 \cite{lean4} is the library everything here depends on. Large formalisations led by mathematicians, the Kepler conjecture \cite{hales2017}, the Liquid Tensor Experiment \cite{scholze2022,castelvecchi2021} and the Fermat's Last Theorem project \cite{fltproject,best2023}, formalise a known proof towards a stated theorem. This edition goes the other way: it starts from statements scattered through drafts and asks which of them can be checked at all. Formal Abstracts \cite{formalabstracts} proposed to state the main results of published papers formally without their proofs; Formal Conjectures \cite{formalconjectures} does this for open problems with a cited source. The statements here have no published source of their own, which is why the attribution rule of Section~\ref{sec:attribution} is needed.

Old mathematical texts have been formalised before. Beeson, Narboux and Wiedijk checked the propositions of Book~I of Euclid's \emph{Elements} by computer \cite{beeson2019}; there, as here, the formal statement is a reading of the text, but Euclid's statements are fixed and published, whereas these are found in drafts by a machine reading. Kohlhase's s\TeX{} uses \LaTeX{} macros as semantic markup for mathematics \cite{kohlhase2008}; the restricted \LaTeX{} of the transcriptions uses macros for editorial semantics (illegible, struck, supplied) and leaves the mathematics unannotated, so that the formal statement is the first place where its meaning is fixed.

Autoformalisation was set as a goal by Szegedy \cite{szegedy2020}. Wu et al.\ \cite{wu2022} showed that large language models can translate competition problems into formal statements, ProofNet \cite{azerbayev2023} gave a benchmark at undergraduate level, and DeepSeek-Prover \cite{xin2024} trained provers on synthetic formal data. These works measure translation and proof on curated statements. Here the statements come from manuscripts read by a model, so a failure can lie in the transcription, the reading, the formal statement or the mathematics, and only the last two are within Lean's reach.

Mathematicians' Nachlässe have been edited by hand, as the \emph{Correspondance Grothendieck--Serre} \cite{colmez2001}, \emph{Les Dérivateurs} \cite{kmm} and \emph{À la poursuite des champs} \cite{maltsiniotis2022} were. The edition's transcription method and its encoding in TEI are reported separately \cite{huajtei}.

\section{Limits and Future Work}\label{sec:limits}

Most of the open statements are out of reach until Mathlib has the theories they speak of, and the edition will not replace them by special cases of its own choosing. While the reading views of the transcriptions are withheld, a reader can check a theorem against the facsimile only through the statement and page reference that each card gives. The \cntDrawings{} drawings that the transcriptions describe in prose (issue \#73) are not yet encoded, and some statements depend on them. The tooling, built for this fonds and reused for other archives, among them the Bourbaki archive and Quillen's notebooks, could become a generic tool for editing mathematical manuscripts into a checked edition (issue \#79). The checks still missing are a specialist's reading of each formal statement against its page, and of the edition's own arguments.
