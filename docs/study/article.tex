\begin{abstract}
\noindent This article reports the transcription by a large language model of the working notes of the mathematician Alexandre Grothendieck (1928--2014), held by the University of Montpellier: 178 openly accessible folders, 16,074 digitised pages, 1949 to 1991, in a fast hand and a notation of his own. Twenty folders are already edited by others. The model transcribed the other 158, twenty pages at a time, into a restricted LaTeX with nine apparatus macros. A converter derives from each file a TEI document in which the mathematics is carried as TeX inside \texttt{<formula>} and commutative diagrams as \texttt{tikz-cd} inside \texttt{<figure>}. A second register, a modernised reading in current notation, is derived from the transcription and never from the hand. The edition stores no page of the fonds: the facsimile beside each reading is Montpellier's own file, passed through a relay. In all, 632 passes have read 9,422 pages in 164 folders, and the apparatus records 67,269 words left illegible, 6.9 per cent of the words met. The article sets out the encoding and its reasons, the registers of names, dates, letters and hands derived from the transcription, and the limits. No batch has been checked by a person, and only individual points have been read against the facsimile. One macro carried two senses. TEI's formula element carries mathematics without describing it. The use of generative AI is declared.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords.} mathematical manuscripts; large language models; transcription; critical apparatus; mathematical notation; archival rights; Alexandre Grothendieck.

\section{Introduction}

Alexandre Grothendieck handed his mathematical papers to his former student Jean Malgoire in two parts, in the summer of 1990 and on 28 July 1995. Malgoire kept them until 2010, deposited them at the University of Montpellier, and gave them to it in 2012 (Université de Montpellier n.d.). The university catalogued the fonds in 2015 and 2016 and has digitised part of it: 178 folders in open access, 16,074 pages by the inventory's count, in twenty-two groups in the order he filed them. They are working notes. He wrote for himself, in ink and pencil, over and between his own lines. He took the same chapter up four times, abandoned a proof and resumed it thirty pages later, and wrote on the backs of administrative letters, seminar notes and other people's typescripts.

Mathematicians have transcribed some of it by hand, over years. Their work covers twenty folders and 5,129 pages, a third of the open pages, in five works. \emph{Les Dérivateurs} is transcribed whole into LaTeX (Künzer, Malgoire and Maltsiniotis n.d.; five folders, 2,135 pages), and so is the \emph{Esquisse d'un programme} (Grothendieck 1997; one folder, 313 pages). Three works are edited in part: the first thirty-seven sections of the \emph{Longue Marche à travers la théorie de Galois} (Grothendieck 1995; four folders, 1,584 pages), the first volume of \emph{À la poursuite des champs} (Maltsiniotis 2022; eight folders, 837 pages), and an English translation of the notes for EGA~V (Grothendieck Circle n.d.; two folders, 260 pages). The correspondence with Serre (Colmez and Serre 2001) edits letters of which the folders hold a few. What he printed, EGA in the \emph{Publications mathématiques de l'IHÉS}, now on Numdam (Grothendieck and Dieudonné 1960--1967), the SGA and the FGA, is the text some folders draft, and is not a transcription of them. The other 158 folders, 10,945 pages, had no transcription, and most of their pages had not been read since they were written.

This article reports an experiment in reading them with a large language model as the first transcriber, under constraints designed to make its reading checkable. It is the second report of a method first applied to the ledgers of Edward and Josephine Hopper (Hua 2026). Three things differ here. The document is mathematics, which is a notation as much as a language, so the encoding of mathematics in TEI becomes a central question. The corpus is two orders of magnitude larger, and the human check becomes the main fact about the edition. And the witness cannot be copied. The fonds is under copyright, a third of its pages may not be circulated, and the edition holds none of it.

The objective is methodological, and no argument here depends on a claim about Grothendieck's mathematics. As in the first report, a reading is \emph{reproducible} when the process that made it can be rerun, \emph{auditable} when a reader can find the evidence for each sentence, \emph{reliable} when like cases are treated alike, and \emph{valid} when it is right. This edition claims the first three and not the fourth.

\section{Related work}

The transcription of handwriting by large language models is a recent field. Humphries and colleagues (2025) report character error rates of six to seven per cent for general-purpose models on eighteenth- and nineteenth-century English manuscripts. Crosilla, Klic and Colavizza (2025) benchmark several models and discuss when a model's linguistic prior helps and when it hinders. Mathematics is an extreme case of the second. A model that has read much mathematics completes a formula as readily as a sentence, and the completed formula is well-formed, so nothing marks it as wrong. It may supply the index that makes an identity true instead of the index on the page. The edition's main rule, that nothing illegible is guessed even where the mathematics would supply it, is written against this prior, and Section 9 measures its cost.

Strutz (2026) proposes a framework for evaluating model-generated TEI, from syntactic validity to semantic accuracy. As in the first report, the model here writes no TEI. It writes a restricted language whose macros correspond one to one with TEI constructs, and the TEI is derived. The precedent for a reduced language converted into conformant TEI is Cummings and Willcox (2013), whose \texttt{tei\_corset} bounded what a keying company could write. Here the macros bound what a model may write, and the converter must be testable.

The Guidelines provide \texttt{<formula>}, whose \texttt{@notation} names the notation of its content, and leave that content to another vocabulary: MathML, TeX, or an image (TEI Consortium 2026). MathML is the W3C's vocabulary for mathematical structure and presentation (W3C 2014), and a TEI file may embed it. Section 6 explains why this edition carries TeX instead, and what that choice costs. Hand attribution, which the first report treated at length, matters less here. The fonds is in his hand, except for other people's letters and typescripts, and those are identified by what they are rather than by their letterforms (Stokes 2015).

\section{The fonds}

\subsection{The inventory, and the three numberings}

The inventory is complete and rigorous, and was made for an archive. Each folder has a shelfmark (\texttt{19}, \texttt{140-2}, \texttt{156-9}), a title, a dating and a page count. The title is his, pencilled on the cover, or the archivists', in square brackets where the folder bore none. The folders stand in twenty-two groups, from \emph{Cristaux} and \emph{Théorie des motifs} to the \emph{Longue marche à travers la théorie de Galois} and \emph{Dérivateurs}. Each folder is served as one PDF of 4 to 270 megabytes.

Three numberings overlap on every page. Grothendieck paginated some runs himself: the Long March runs from 1 to 787 across four boxes. The archivists pencilled a number at the foot of every page, and the inventory counts these; the edition calls them pages. The PDF's numbering is one ahead of the archivists', because the university's file opens on a generated cover. The edition addresses a page by the archivists' number and converts to the PDF's only when it requests a page from Montpellier.

\subsection{What the pages are}

The pages are drafts, and restarts are transcribed like any other page. A verso is often unrelated to its recto (an administrative letter, a typescript returned by a secretary) and was digitised deliberately, sometimes upside down. Most dates are inferred. Figure~\ref{fig:dating} sets the inventory's datings against the dates the transcriptions have found on the leaves. Of the 153 folders the inventory dates, 111 rest in part on a deduction the archivists mark with square brackets, and 25 more are \q{s.d.}, undated. The transcriptions have found 199 dates on the leaves, in 64 folders. Forty-seven of them are machine stamps on the computer listings whose backs he wrote on in the 1980s, and these date the paper, not the writing. A reading of the facsimiles of eight edited folders adds 40 more, eight of them stamps.

\section{The transcription method}

\input{fig-pipeline.tex}

\subsection{The schema, and its values here}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} gives the arrangement described in the first report. A \emph{witness} is held by the institution and never committed. A \emph{reading} is the one file that is written, by the model first and by a person afterwards, in a language whose every construct is declared. \emph{Derivations} are computed from the reading by scripts on every build. \emph{Verifications} are whatever a machine can check of them. An \emph{address} places the reading beside its witness and cites it by the holder's reference and the edition's dated pass. The person closes the loop. What is new here is a model that also restates, a witness that cannot be mirrored for the reader, and a notation.

\subsection{The unit of work and the model}

A batch is twenty of the archivists' pages, read in one fresh model context and written as a single file, \texttt{transcripts/<folder>/batch-NN.fr.tex}. Beyond twenty pages of this hand, the reading degrades towards the end of the pass, and nothing in the output signals where. The skill that performs the reading admits named models only. Each file's header records the model and the date in a fixed form, \texttt{\% Pass: Opus 5.5 (claude-opus-5-5), <date> --- first pass, unchecked against the pages by a human}, so that provenance is recorded in the file itself. Of the 632 first passes, 499 were read by Claude Opus 5.5, 102 by Claude Opus 5, 26 by Claude Fable 5.1 and 5 by Claude Fable 5. The Fable models are no longer used for new passes, and the headers of the files they read say so. Fifty files in twenty-six folders carry a later revision below the first pass, dated and attributed in the same form; some apply readings the editor settled on the facsimile, and none makes its batch checked. The batches of a folder may run in parallel. No batch then sees the one before it, and each header says where a run of text crosses its boundary.

Every pass receives two aids, and neither may settle a reading. The first is a set of tiles: each page cut into six overlapping crops at 700 dpi, with the margin turned upright, so that the pass spends no effort choosing what to look at. The second is a lexicon of his vocabulary, abbreviations and notation, counted over all transcriptions and regenerated after each run. It separates words read plainly from words read with doubt. On a hard leaf a pass usually chooses between candidates, and the lexicon shows which belongs to this material. Built from unverified transcriptions, it makes no claim about ink. It records that \emph{principal} is common in these pages, and leaves to the page whether one stroke reads \emph{principaux}.

\subsection{The rules}

The rule of the Hopper edition applies with a sharper consequence. An invented word in a letter is a bad reading, and an invented index in a formula can state a result nobody proved. Nothing illegible is guessed, and the mark for an illegible word is never replaced by what the mathematics would require. A doubtful reading is marked as doubtful. His spelling, punctuation, long sentences and slips are kept, and slips are flagged, not corrected. The transcriber's notes are in French, so that a French reader meets one language throughout.

The transcription is not diplomatic, and says so. The material apparatus of the archive (which verso carried which letter, where a separator sheet fell, which page was scanned upside down) is not its subject. A page with no mathematics is omitted, and the gap in the page numbers records it. Where a page is too crowded to render whole, the mathematics is transcribed and the surrounding prose compressed, with a note. Commutative diagrams go into \texttt{tikz-cd}, the notation in which this mathematics is read today. His prose stays whenever it concerns the mathematics, as when he explains why a definition is the right one. What he struck out stays, marked, because a deletion shows where the argument changed.

Three rules concern other people. A typescript or mémoire by someone else is given as a note per page, with his marks on it in full. A letter to him is transcribed in full where its writer is a public figure, and otherwise summarised with the writer unnamed. Letters already published in the correspondence with Serre are condensed to their statements and a summary. Each rule answers a right the edition does not hold.

\subsection{What the model writes}

The model writes a restricted LaTeX. Its apparatus is nine macros (Table~\ref{tab:macros}). The mathematics is ordinary LaTeX mathematics, inline and displayed, with the environments a working mathematician uses and \texttt{tikz-cd} for diagrams. Six declarations open each file: the shelfmark, the batch, its pages, the inventory's dating copied with its brackets, the folder's title, and a watermark, \emph{Édition de démonstration}, which the PDF prints across every page.

\section{Two registers}

The Hopper ledgers needed one text. This fonds needs two, because of the notation. A \(\mathcal{C}\) of 1962 is not drawn like one of 1986, a sheaf of the Séminaire is not named as sheaves are named today, and a reader who meets \q{fppf} spelled out on one page and reduced to initials on the next needs to be told it is the same topology. Every folder read whole therefore yields a second document, the \emph{modernised reading}. It opens with a résumé for a reader new to the subject, then gives the mathematics in current notation and current names, with footnotes carrying everything the transcription's apparatus carried.

Three decisions govern it. The modernised reading is derived from the transcription and never from the hand, since two independent readings of one hand would diverge with nothing to decide between them. It is made per folder, because his arguments do not stop where the batches do. And it is the one document allowed to depart from the page, so it is held to another standard. It must be correct as it stands. Where the manuscript is loose, the reading states what is true and gives what the page has in a footnote. The skill states the standard as four failure modes to check: a statement strengthened beyond what the page proves, a hypothesis dropped, a notation modernised into a different object, and a gap closed silently.

The résumé ends with a line of English keywords, the modern vocabulary to search under. They are the only part of either register written for search. The site's search, its index of subjects and its map of the mathematics are built from them (Section 7.3), and the site labels them as the reading's. The 160 folders read whole in this way carry 3,272 keywords.

\section{The TEI encoding}

\subsection{A derived export}

A script exports every transcription to TEI P5. It reads the \texttt{.tex} and nothing else, neither the facsimile nor the rendered HTML, and its mapping is that of Table~\ref{tab:macros}. The reasons are those of the first report. A model asked for TEI writes it fluently, and well-formed markup with an invented attribute shows no structural sign of the error. A nine-macro language has a small surface, and each macro answers one editorial question. Because the TEI is derived, any difference between a file and its TEI is a bug in the converter. The LaTeX is legible only to this repository; TEI can be taken in by an archive or another editor without the edition's tools.

The header carries the file's comment in machine-readable form. The responsibility statement names the model as a \texttt{<name type="model">}, which every editorial note points to. The availability is \texttt{restricted}, with the reasons: the fonds is under copyright, the transcription is an unauthorised demonstration, and no image is reproduced. The manuscript description gives the shelfmark, the inventory's title, and its dating in an \texttt{<origDate>}. The surrogate is Montpellier's PDF, with a note that each \texttt{@facs} points one page ahead of its \texttt{@n}. Every pass recorded in the source is a \texttt{<change>} in \texttt{<revisionDesc>}, dated and pointing to its model.

\subsection{Mathematics as TeX}

Mathematics is carried as TeX, unchanged, inside \texttt{<formula notation="TeX">}, with displayed formulas marked by \texttt{@rend}. A commutative diagram is a \texttt{<figure type="diagram">} holding a \texttt{<formula notation="tikz-cd">}. The few drawings that an argument depends on and that are not arrow graphs (arcs, chains of faces, pseudo-lines) are redrawn in TikZ and carried as \texttt{<figure type="picture">}. It holds the source in a \texttt{<formula notation="TikZ">} and, in a \texttt{<graphic>}, an SVG compiled from it, since no browser draws TikZ. Neither TEI nor the converter encodes the mathematics. There are three reasons for this, and one cost.

First, the transcription is already TeX, and TeX is closer to what he wrote than MathML would be. He wrote a notation and did not declare a structure: an underlined \(\omega\), a doubly underlined \(\Sigma\), a tilde that distinguishes two coverings, one letter used for two objects on one page. Presentation MathML would be a second transcription of the first, and content MathML would be an interpretation, which belongs to the modernised reading. Second, the apparatus. An illegible exponent or a doubtful index is an apparatus mark inside a formula, written as \texttt{\textbackslash ill\{\}} inside \texttt{\$…\$}. Kept as TeX, it stays where both the browser's renderer (KaTeX) and the PDF read it. A conversion to MathML would have to give an illegible subscript a structure, which nobody knows. Third, the round trip: the TeX inside the TEI is the TeX of the source, character for character, so the two can be compared as strings.

The cost is that the TEI says nothing about the mathematics that a TEI processor can use. A reader of the TEI can find every formula but cannot search inside one, and the 1,990 commutative diagrams are opaque to the schema. An apparatus mark inside a formula is invisible to TEI: a count of \texttt{<gap>} elements misses every illegible index. The edition's own counts (Section 7.3) are therefore taken from the source. MathML can be derived later from the same TeX, where the TeX is clean enough, without touching the transcription. The journal's own customisation, against which this article is encoded, admits no \texttt{<formula>}, so the article's TEI carries its few inline symbols as Unicode.

\subsection{Validation}

The edition has a customisation of its own, modelled on the one for the Hopper ledgers, where a corpus of well-formed files had turned out to be invalid because nothing had checked them (Hua 2026). The ODD declares the sixty-four elements the converter emits, header included. It closes the value lists of the attributes that carry meaning: \texttt{@type} on \texttt{<div>}, \texttt{<figure>} and \texttt{<note>}, \texttt{@notation} on \texttt{<formula>} (\texttt{TeX}, \texttt{tikz-cd}, \texttt{TikZ}), \texttt{@reason} on \texttt{<gap>}, and \texttt{@rend} wherever the converter writes it. It is a pure subset, so a file valid against it is valid TEI. 

The RELAX NG schema is derived from the ODD on every build and published beside it, and every exported file names it in an \texttt{xml-model} processing instruction. The build validates every file with jing against unmodified \texttt{tei\_all} and against the derived schema, with every input pinned by version and digest. It also compares the ODD with the export in both directions and fails on an element used but not declared, or declared but never used. A further check typesets every formula of the TEI's reading view and of the \texttt{.tex}'s with KaTeX and compares them page by page. Two things are not validated: the Schematron rules that \texttt{tei\_all} embeds, which are not run over the corpus, and the TeX inside each \texttt{<formula>}, which the schema sees as a string and only KaTeX reads.

\section{Interface and reproducibility}

\subsection{The facsimile}

The site at \url{https://grothendieck.commutator.io} shows the transcription in one pane and Montpellier's facsimile in the other. Scrolling the transcription past a page break turns the facsimile to that page. A reading is addressed by page: \texttt{\#140-1/2/p28} opens folder \texttt{140-1} at batch 2, scrolled to page 28, with the facsimile at the same page. Every register below links in that form. The edition holds no page of the fonds, either in the repository, whose ignore file refuses PDF files, or on the server.

A frame pointed at Montpellier's server fails, for three reasons. The server sends the header \texttt{X-Frame-Options: SAMEORIGIN}; its certificate expired on 10 December 2025, and it sends no cross-origin headers. The browser enforces all three against the remote origin; none applies to a request made by a server. A relay on the edition's origin therefore requests the file and passes the bytes through unchanged, with range requests, so that opening page 221 of a 204-megabyte folder transfers 256 kilobytes. The relay serves only an allowlist of the folders' files, checked against the inventory, and stores nothing.

The reading can therefore be checked against its witness only while Montpellier's server is online. When it is down, the pane says so and offers no copy; the local mirror that the passes read from is never served.

\subsection{Counts computed from the files}

Everything the site shows beyond the transcription is derived from it, and every count is computed from the files. The manifest of what is transcribed is built from the files present. A batch counts as transcribed once its LaTeX exists, and as modernised once the folder's reading exists; neither status claims that anyone has checked it. The site's progress figure is computed against the folders nobody has edited, and the figure against the whole fonds is printed beside it.

\subsection{Registers derived from the reading}

Because the transcription is written in a constrained language, a question put to the fonds can be answered by a script. The edition publishes seven such registers. Each is regenerated from the files, and each entry rests on a file and a line.

\emph{The hand} counts the apparatus per batch and per folder. Of the words met, 910,954 were read and 67,269 left illegible, 6.9 per cent; 48,663 readings were marked doubtful. He struck 39,047 times and inserted 14,300 times outside the transcriber's notes; the transcriber supplied 768 letters or words; there are 5,772 marginal notes, 1,990 commutative diagrams and 901 drawings, four of them redrawn and the others described in notes. Figure~\ref{fig:hand} places each folder's share of unread words against its inventory dating. The count is of the apparatus. An \texttt{\textbackslash ill\{\}} is a word one pass could not read, so the count depends on the pass as well as on the hand.

\input{fig-hand.tex}

\emph{The timeline} draws each folder's inventory dating as a range, dark where the archivists read it and light where they deduced it, with the dates on the leaves as dots (Figure~\ref{fig:dating} summarises it). The parser keeps the qualifier of each end of a dating, \q{vers}, \q{à partir de}, \q{avant}; reducing \q{1965-[vers 1971]} to 1965--1971 would lose the distinction the page records. \emph{The letters} is a register of 76 letters in 33 folders, 1959 to 1991, each with its date as written, its direction, its form, and how the transcription gives it, private correspondents unnamed. \emph{The index} leads from a person, a work of his cited by volume (EGA, SGA, FGA), or a subject, to the folder and batch where it occurs. \emph{The maps} are co-occurrence networks in the manner of bibliometric mapping: who is named together in a folder, which subjects share folders, which folders cite which volumes. Every edge rests on evidence, and the layout uses a fixed seed. A slider shows each map with only the folders dated by a given year, by the midpoint of the inventory's dating.

The last two registers collect the mathematics itself. \emph{The diagrams} are the 1,990 \texttt{tikzcd} environments of the transcriptions, in 142 folders, shown one to a slide by the reading view's renderer. \emph{The formulas} are the 25,920 displayed formulas, in 156 folders, set by KaTeX with their apparatus. Each register opens on its richest items, ranked by a stated count: the distinct symbols a diagram or formula uses (a letter in a given alphabet, so that \(\mathcal{C}\) is not \(C\), a named symbol, an operator), plus nodes and arrows for a diagram and size for a formula. The ranking measures quantity and says nothing of importance. The richest diagram has nineteen objects and twenty-nine arrows, on page 28 of folder \texttt{140-1}, a draft of the \emph{Longue Marche}.

Two rules govern these registers. The transcriber's notes are excluded from every count of his words, since a note discussing a notion would otherwise report it as his. A person counts as a node where the folder holds a letter to or from them, a text of theirs, or his naming them as the source of a result, as in \q{th. de Hodge} or \q{d'après Deligne}. A mathematical object that bears a name, such as \q{groupe de Galois}, does not count. The test is adjacency in the text. A source judged unreliable is not used, even where it is the only source for a claim; the claim then stays unsourced and the graph omits it.

\section{Findings}

Reading a folder end to end turns up things the inventory does not record: a leaf bound backwards, two manuscripts interleaved, a theorem proved under a hypothesis weaker than the one in print. The edition keeps them in a list designed to prevent overclaiming. A claim that something in these folders is new is a claim about the literature, which can be searched but never exhausted. A claim that it came first needs a date, which the folders mostly lack.

Each row carries a status from a closed list: \emph{unsearched}, nobody has looked it up; \emph{candidate}, searched and not found; \emph{matched}, found in the literature and kept; \emph{refuted}, shown false as stated by a counterexample, kept with what still stands; \emph{confirmed}, which only a person may set. Every row names the sources searched and separates what the page carries from what the edition supplied. The skill that proposes rows reads the transcription and the modernised reading, never the facsimile, and may not assert priority. Matched and refuted rows stay in the list, so the next reader is spared the search. The list holds 444 rows from 151 folders: 217 unsearched, 151 candidates, 67 matched, nine refuted, and none confirmed. Statements of the readings of twenty-two folders are also proved in Lean~4 against mathlib, in 397 theorems using only Lean's standard axioms. A proof shows that the reading's statement holds, which does not make it what the page says; in some folders it found a statement false as given.

One row shows the purpose of the list. Folder 27 holds five typed leaves headed in pencil \q{Exp XIII}: a proposition on a group acting on a scheme, with a chain of implications among six conditions, and three corollaries. A first pass summarised them as an already published typescript. A comparison with the exposé in the current re-edition of SGA~3 (Gille and Polo 2008--2011, Exposé XIII in its version of December 2008) showed the premise was wrong. The published exposé goes from its first lemma directly to its second section, and the general statements reappear there only in specialised form. The leaves are an earlier, longer version that is not in print, and they were re-transcribed in full. This does not establish that the version matters. It establishes that the inventory's \q{typescript} and the published exposé are different texts, which a reader of the folder could not have known.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{The reading against the image}

The transcription is a first pass, and no batch has been checked by a person. The editor has checked individual points against the facsimile: dates, doubtful words, the hand of an addition, the ink of typed batches sampled one page each, untranscribed pages (found blank), the sixty-three points at which ten documents differ from a transcription made independently elsewhere, and a few pages of letters and typescripts. Forty-three batch files record such checks, each dated and signed in the file, and none makes its batch checked. The page of folder 19 in Section 4.5 was read against the facsimile in this way, and two of its readings were corrected (Appendix A). The edition's status file, in which only a person may declare a batch checked or skipped, declares no batch checked and two skipped, and the site shows every batch as drafted. The rate at which the reading fails is stated in the apparatus: 6.9 per cent of words left illegible and a further 5.3 readings in a hundred words marked doubtful, varying by folder from nothing to more than a third (Figure~\ref{fig:hand}). These are not error rates. A word misread plainly leaves no mark, and only a person with the facsimile can find it.

\subsection{Limits of the model as a transcriber and as an encoder}

There are five limits, none of them an error rate.

\emph{One macro carried two senses.} \texttt{\textbackslash add} was first declared as the transcriber's addition (a missing letter, an implied word) and mapped to \texttt{<supplied resp="\#pass">}. In context, most of its uses were his: a word written above the line with a caret, a substitution over a struck word, the \(\alpha_i\) of Section 4.5. The TEI thus credited several thousand of his insertions to the model. The Hopper edition had the same fault and split the macro in two, and this edition did the same. \texttt{\textbackslash supplied} now carries the transcriber's sense and maps to \texttt{<supplied resp="\#pass">}; \texttt{\textbackslash add} carries his and maps to \texttt{<add>}. The existing uses were divided by a rule that can be checked in the file, without a new reading: an addition attached to the word it completes, with a letter or digit immediately before or after it, is the transcriber's, and a free-standing one is his. The rule assigned 529 of the 6,144 uses to \texttt{\textbackslash supplied} and left 5,615 as \texttt{\textbackslash add}; later passes use the two macros from the start. The rule's output is a guess. A closing parenthesis restored at a clipped edge still reads as his, and a prefix he inserted, now \q{\texttt{\textbackslash supplied\{quasi-\}torseur}}, is attributed to the transcriber. Only a person reading the facsimiles can settle these cases. The general lesson is that a macro whose declared sense is narrower than its natural sense will be used in the natural one.

\emph{The model knows the mathematics.} Its prior is the hazard the rule of Section 4.3 addresses, and the rule holds only where it is applied. An illegible index that the context determines is easy to fill in. The transcriptions write \texttt{\textbackslash ill\{\}} and, where the context fixes the value, say so in a note, as in \q{l'indice de \(k\) est un pâté \ldots\ le contexte ne le fixe pas}. A pass that supplied the index silently would leave no trace. The method separates the registers for this reason. Completion is allowed in the modernised reading, which may state what is true, and forbidden in the transcription.

\emph{The apparatus is inside the mathematics.} As Section 6.2 explains, a count over the TEI misses every mark inside a formula, and a user of the TEI alone would undercount the illegible words.

\emph{Other people's texts.} The fonds holds letters, typescripts and mémoires by others, some of them private persons. The rules of Section 4.3 answer to rights, and the model has to apply them. The skill judges conservatively whether a writer is a public figure, and fourteen of the seventy-six records in the register of letters leave the correspondent unnamed. Only a human reviewer can check that judgement. An error there would disclose a private person's text.

\emph{Dates.} The model reads a date as readily as a word, and dates are what the edition most needs, since the inventory's datings are mostly inferred (Figure~\ref{fig:dating}). The register of dated leaves distinguishes a date in his hand from a typist's and a machine's, keeps two-digit years as written, and fixes a year from a neighbouring leaf only where the run is continuous, saying so. Of its 247 records, 207 were assembled by a model reading the transcriptions and have not been checked against the facsimile.

\subsection{Human validation, and the pace}

\input{fig-pace.tex}

Figure~\ref{fig:pace} gives the pace from the repository's history. The first batch file was committed on 8 August 2026 and the 632nd on 10 October: 43 in August, 388 in September and 201 in October, with up to eight passes running at once. The 618 batches of the folders no edition covers were transcribed in about eight weeks. The human check takes far longer. A mathematician who can read this hand needs about an afternoon to compare a batch of twenty pages line by line with the facsimile. The 618 batches therefore represent some two and a half thousand hours of a rare kind of reader. The checking, and no longer the transcription, limits the edition.

The protocol the edition proposes is the one its status file already encodes. A person opens a batch beside its facsimile, reads each transcribed line against the page, corrects the transcription at source with a note saying who checked it, and only then declares the batch checked. Until then every citation names a machine pass and a date, and the address of a page does not change when its reading does.

\subsection{What the method permits, and what it does not}

The method allows one person and a model to produce, in months, a reading of a sixteen-thousand-page mathematical archive, cited by folder, batch, page and pass, exported to TEI with its mathematics intact, placed beside the institution's facsimile without copying it, and mined for registers that each rest on a line. It does not produce a checked transcription, it does not encode the mathematics in a form a TEI processor can reason over, and it cannot decide with authority who is a public figure.

\section{Conclusion}

This article has described a second edition built on the method of the first, and what a corpus of mathematics changes in it. The notation required a decision the ledgers did not. Mathematics is carried inside \texttt{<formula>} as the TeX it was transcribed in, without a structure the transcription does not have, and the interpretation of the notation goes into a second register derived from the first. The size of the corpus made the human check the main fact about the edition. The rights meant that the edition points to the facsimile instead of serving it, through a relay that stores nothing.

The encoding is in place and validated on every build. Two pieces of work remain. The first is to check on the facsimile the insertions that the rule of Section 9.2 divided mechanically between him and the transcriber. The second is the human check of every batch. Every claim of the edition depends on it, and it requires mathematicians who can read this hand.

For this fonds, checking means reading Grothendieck's hand against sixteen thousand pages. Few people can do it, and nobody can do it alone. The method provides a cited reading before that check, and does not replace it.
