# Reading the hand

What passes have learned about Grothendieck's handwriting, so that the next one
does not learn it again. Every batch so far has re-derived the same half-dozen
facts from scratch, at the cost of the attention they were meant to save.

**How to use this file, and how not to.** It narrows a field of candidates for a
stroke you cannot read. It never settles a reading. Nothing here is evidence
about a particular page: if the guide says a certain stroke is usually *X* and
the page in front of you will not support *X*, the page wins and the apparatus
says so. `\ill{}` remains the correct answer whenever it is the true one, and a
guide that talks a pass out of `\ill{}` has done harm, not good.

**Most of what follows is itself unverified.** It is distilled from first-pass
transcriptions, **none of which has been checked against the leaves by a human**
— `transcripts/status.json` records the checked ones and there are none. Read
the examples as "a pass settled on this", not as "this is what the page says".

See also `lexicon.md`, generated by `npm run lexicon`, for frequency counts of
his vocabulary, his abbreviations and the notation of the fonds.

---

## 1. Get the page in front of you properly first

Most "illegible" is under-magnified. Before deciding a word is lost:

```
npm run tiles -- <folder> <batch>
```

It writes `archives/tiles/<folder>/p<page>/` — the sheet whole, six overlapping
tiles at 700 dpi, the left margin turned upright, and `tiles.json` with the
rectangles so a doubtful word can be re-cropped tighter without guessing
coordinates again:

```
pdftoppm -png -r 1100 -f <pdfPage> -l <pdfPage> -singlefile \
  -x <x> -y <y> -W <w> -H <h> archives/batches/<folder>/batch-NN.pdf out
```

**The scans are ~229 ppi native**, so a 700 dpi tile adds no information that is
not already on the film. It adds *legibility*, which is a different thing and
the thing that matters. A pass on folder 29 concluded from the true premise that
higher-resolution crops were pointless; that conclusion is wrong in practice and
cost several pages. Both halves of it are recorded here so the argument stops.

**Turn the margin.** He rotates the sheet and writes up the left edge. A note
read sideways is a note not read — folder 29 page 169 carries a marginal note on
`π̃₁(S,ξ)` that a pass left `\ill{}` and that is legible once turned. `npm run
tiles` emits `margin-1.png` … upright.

**Turn the whole leaf, too.** An unrelated verso was often scanned in whatever
orientation preserved the physical object. A page that looks like nothing may be
upside down.

## 2. His shorthand

Half of how he writes, and what most defeats a reader coming to the hand cold.
`lexicon.md` has the counted list; these are the ones that cause trouble.

| Written | Reads as | Note |
|---|---|---|
| `tt` | tout, tous, toute | very frequent, easily read as `tl`, `H` |
| `tjs` | toujours | |
| `p. ex.` | par exemple | |
| `loc. noeth.` | localement noethérien | the second word looks like *modh.*, *moetli.* |
| `fid. plat` | fidèlement plat | often with `qu.-cpt` |
| `qu.-cpt`, `q.c.` | quasi-compact | |
| `alg. clos` | algébriquement clos | *k alg. clos* can look like `h ny. chs` |
| `gpes`, `s-gpes` | groupes, sous-groupes | |
| `pt`, `pts` | point, points | usually with `géom.` |
| `cqfd` | ce qu'il fallait démontrer | closes a proof; a reliable landmark |
| `N.B.` | nota bene | opens an aside, often the honest one |
| `Ex.` | exemple | |
| `s.d.` | sans date | the archivists', not his |
| `½` | semi- | `½cosimplicial` = *semi-cosimplicial*; folder 29 pp. 184-185 |

## 3. Strokes that mislead, with the pages they were fought on

Read the right-hand column as a candidate to test, never as a key.

| The stroke looks like | A pass settled on | Where |
|---|---|---|
| `p—uncaux`, `p—unimaux` | *principaux* | folder 29 p. 170, classing covering families |
| `h ny. chs` | *k alg. clos* | folder 29 p. 161 |
| `loc. modh.` | *loc. noeth.* | folder 29 p. 161, interlinear |
| `co—o—gan` | *conoyau* | folder 29 p. 161 |
| `vt. un` | *sur un* | folder 29 p. 161 |
| `pui. le ss` | *privé de sa* | folder 29 p. 161, before *p-composante* |
| a long squiggle ending `-ion` | *injection*, *réunion*, *intersection* | folder 29 pp. 164, 169 |

**The lesson these have in common is not palaeographic.** Every one was settled
by the surrounding mathematics, not by the ink: *principaux* because the lines
around it are classing covering families and that is the word that occurs there.
When a stroke will not resolve, read the argument, not the letters.

## 4. Ambiguous glyphs

- **Roman `I` against `2`.** Near-identical. Folder 29 p. 175 writes
  `π^I(X,G) ≃ H²(X̌,G)` four lines above an unambiguous `π²`, and the
  transcription keeps the `I` with a `\note{}` rather than normalising it.
  **Do not silently regularise a superscript**; flag the discrepancy.
- **Superscript `ξ` against a filled blob.** The same stroke. Folder 29 p. 180
  runs `X_i` over `X_i^ξ` over `K_i` over `k_i` and the superscripts have to be
  read off the argument.
- **`ʒ` / `𝔷`.** A curly figure-3 for a group he does not otherwise name
  (folder 29 p. 176). Transcribe as `\mathfrak{z}` and note it once.
- **His `s` can be read as a `2`.** Folder 29 pages 184-185 write « foncteur
  ½cosimplicial » in a hand that reads "½co2-indicat" — the `s` of *-simplicial*
  taken for a 2, and the `½` not yet recognised as *semi-*. What settled it was
  not the ink but the diagram the word names, $F^0 \rightrightarrows F^1
  \Rrightarrow F^2$: coface maps and no degeneracies is exactly a
  semi-cosimplicial object. Read the argument, not the letters.
- **Doubled indices.** He often writes the same index as both subscript and
  superscript (`X_1^1`, `K_2^2`, folder 29 p. 180). Transcribe as written.
- **`\mathcal{C}` changes shape across the decades.** Fix the reading once per
  batch and hold to it.

## 5. How he lays a run out

- **He paginates his own runs, and restarts.** Folder 29 carries `I–VI`
  (pp. 46–57), `1–4` (60–63), `A–L` (65–76), `1–6` (87–97 and 124–129). Where a
  run crosses a batch boundary, record his number in a `\note{}` so a
  cross-reference like « cf. p. 12 » can be followed. Some runs carry no
  pagination at all and are named by their headings instead.
- **An otherwise blank sheet inscribed in his hand is a cover**, naming the run
  that follows. It gets **no `\page{}`** — the gap in the numbering is the
  record that it was skipped. **His words become the section heading**, with a
  `\note{}` saying which page they come off and that they are his. Folder 29
  pp. 111, 123, 143, 163, 172, 174, 177.
- **A typescript can break off mid-sentence at a batch boundary.** Read the tail
  of the previous batch's `.tex` before starting: the notation is fixed there.
- **He restarts a chapter rather than revising it.** Two *moutures* that look
  alike are not a repetition to be merged; their difference is the information.

## 6. Deletions, insertions, and the paper he wrote on

- **Struck text is content.** `\struck{}`. A deletion shows where the thought
  turned. Where a cancelled passage cannot be read, one `\struck{\ill{}}` and
  one `\note{}` for the whole block — not a mark per line.
- **A whole page cancelled by diagonal strokes** still gets transcribed if it
  can be read (folder 29 p. 103 reads cleanly end to end under two long
  diagonals). Record the cancellation once.
- **Interlinear substitution runs through a passage.** Folder 29 p. 157 replaces
  *indécomposable* by *connexe* at every occurrence. Once you see one, look for
  the rest.
- **A typed verso is kept when it carries something in his hand, and skipped
  when it does not.** This is the settled rule, arrived at in batches 5 and 6 of
  folder 29 and applied without argument since.
- **Typescript carbons often have their arrows and Greek letters missing.** The
  machine did not carry them and they were to be inked by hand. Restore where
  the text settles them, mark where it does not, one note at the first
  occurrence per batch.

## 7. English stands in the French, and stays

*wild* (wild inertia), *Main Proposition*, « Kif -- kif » for "these two say the
same thing". Whole runs are in English — the Murre correspondence, his own
reading notes on a typescript. Do not translate, do not flag as foreign.

## 8. What legibility actually tracks

Not the decade. **Whether he was drafting or copying out.** Folder 29 pages
170–171, 173 and 178–179 are written fair and read almost end to end; pages
164–169, four sheets away in the same folder and the same year, are a fast
palimpsest whose formulas carry and whose connective prose does not.

That asymmetry is the normal condition of this material, and it dictates the
shape of an honest transcription: **the mathematical statements come through in
full; the sentences between them are often lost.** Leave them `\ill{}`. A batch
carrying two hundred apparatus marks is not a failed batch — folder 29's batches
8 and 9 both do, and they are the record of what the leaves actually support.
