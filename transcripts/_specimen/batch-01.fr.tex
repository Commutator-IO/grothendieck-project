\begin{summary}
These twenty leaves are about a single question: what is the smallest amount of
structure a space needs before one can do cohomology on it? Grothendieck's
answer, worked out here, is that one does not need points at all.

The problem arises because the spaces of algebraic geometry are badly behaved
in the ordinary topological sense — the Zariski topology has so few open sets
that the usual invariants collapse and say nothing. One wants a notion of
"covering" that is finer than the topology allows.

His idea is to keep the *bookkeeping* of coverings and throw away the points. A
site is a category together with a rule saying which families count as
coverings; a topos is the category of sheaves on such a site. Everything one
actually used points for — restriction, gluing, cohomology — turns out to be
expressible in that bookkeeping alone. If you have met sheaves on a topological
space, this is the same definition with the open sets replaced by arrows.

This is the machinery SGA 4 was built to carry, and it is what makes étale
cohomology — and eventually the Weil conjectures — possible. A reader wanting
the next step should look at the notion of a Grothendieck topology on a
category, then at sheaves valued in sets before sheaves valued in anything else.
\end{summary}
